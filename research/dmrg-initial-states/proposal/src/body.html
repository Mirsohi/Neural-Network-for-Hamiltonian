Research proposal · draft for discussion

Learning the starting state

Hamiltonian-conditioned neural networks that propose symmetry-resolved MPS seeds for faster and more reliable DMRG ground-state searches

Prepared by Kourosh · 13 October 2026

Summary

DMRG is the method of choice for ground states of quasi-one-dimensional systems and cylinders, but on wide cylinders and in frustrated, doped and topological problems its outcome depends on the starting MPS. Competing states separated by 10−3 to 10−7 per site survive to bond dimensions in the thousands, topological sectors are selected rather than discovered, and practitioners fall back on hand-designed pinning fields and multi-start searches.

We propose to train a neural network that takes a Hamiltonian and a target symmetry sector and returns a small set of MPS seeds. From these seeds DMRG should reach the correct ground state more often, and in less total time, than from random, product, mean-field, parton or continuation starts, with all network costs counted. A literature search found no published work that does this and reports measured savings.

The project begins with a calibration study that needs no machine learning: how DMRG time-to-solution depends on the quality and basin of the start. That study is publishable on its own, and it tells us where a learned start can pay off before we train anything.

Motivation: when the starting state matters

DMRG is a local, alternating optimizer {{white1992}}. Because each step only updates one or two site tensors in an environment built from the rest of the MPS, the algorithm “can get stuck in a metastable state,” and “a key way to avoid metastability is to begin with a wavefunction that is already close to the true ground state” {{stoudenmire2012}}. The literature shows two distinct ways the start matters, and only the second one justifies a learned initializer.

Missing structure. A start can lack quantum-number sectors, bond-basis directions or long-range correlations that local updates cannot create. In an extreme test, a 20-site spin-1/2 chain started from an artificial state converged to E = −6.355 instead of −8.682 without subspace enrichment {{hubig2015}}. White’s density-matrix noise {{white2005}}, subspace expansion {{hubig2015}} and controlled bond expansion {{gleis2023}} repair this within a few sweeps. In gapped 1D problems a better start therefore buys a constant factor, and schedule and algorithm tuning alone already deliver 2–6× {{hubig2015,sehlstedt2026}}.

Basin selection. When nearly degenerate states compete, the start decides which one DMRG converges to. The energy differences involved, roughly 10−3 to 10−7 per site, are comparable to or smaller than the finite-bond-dimension error, so the optimizer cannot tell the states apart. Table 1 collects documented cases. This regime is the target of the proposal.

Current practice in these problems is a hand-run multi-start search: seed several candidate patterns with product states and temporary fields, extrapolate each run, and keep the lowest {{zheng2017,qin2020}}. A 2024 study notes that DMRG “can hop among low-lying states depending on the initial condition” {{xu2024}}, and DMRG “preferentially converges to one of the quasidegenerate ground states” {{samajdar2021}}. The same pattern appears at the frontier of quantum chemistry, where a 2026 FeMoco study sampled 78,750 mean-field guesses to choose 840 DMRG references at 317,440 core-hours per sweep {{zhai2026}}. This enumeration, not the cost of early sweeps, is what a learned model should replace.

What exists today

Algorithmic remedies make results independent of the bond basis, not of the basin

Noise, subspace expansion and controlled bond expansion remove the missing-structure failures {{white2005,hubig2015,gleis2023}}, and wavefunction prediction inside each sweep cut Davidson time by 20–50× {{white1996}}. None of these selects a basin. Noise also partly scrambles a good start, which matters when testing any designed seed.

Physics-designed seeds work, but unevenly

Pinning fields and symmetry-broken product states are standard for competing orders, at the price of biasing the outcome and requiring multi-start checks {{stoudenmire2012}}. Gaussian (Slater-determinant) MPS are cheap to build, about 90 s on a laptop for a gapless 1025-site chain {{fishman2015}}, but are barely benchmarked as DMRG seeds, and a user report found them worse than random for a 2D Hubbard model {{itensor755}}.

Gutzwiller-projected parton MPS are the best-quantified case {{jin2021}}. Three findings from that work shape this proposal. A parton seed that had lost about a quarter of its weight to truncation still worked well. A seed fixed to the right sector but otherwise random gave no gain, so the correlation structure matters and not only the quantum numbers. And in the intermediate-field Kitaev regime no ansatz helped. On the kagome lattice the lowest-energy parton ansatz converged slowest {{sun2024}}, and critical SO(5) and SO(6) chains showed no advantage over random starts {{cai2025}}. The energy or fidelity of a start is therefore a weak predictor of time-to-solution.

Parameter continuation, reusing the converged MPS from a neighbouring coupling, is cheap and probably the strongest baseline in smooth regions. It fails across transitions (hysteresis) and offers nothing for a new disorder realization, size or geometry. Rigorous-RG seeds fixed the failures in Table 1’s 1D chains but cost about 500× more than DMRG itself {{block2021}}.

Machine learning has not yet been used to generate DMRG starting states

A search covering 2017–2026 found no paper in which a neural network or other ML model generates the DMRG starting MPS and reports sweep or wall-clock savings. Full-text search coverage was incomplete, so we treat this as likely rather than certain. The closest items are:

  • a 2019 APS March Meeting talk claiming ML-accelerated DMRG convergence, with no method or numbers and no follow-up paper {{marks2019}};
  • a 2026 study in which an LLM coding agent tuned DMRG protocols under a 20 s budget and switched the start from random to Néel-type product states, chosen from a fixed list {{mantilla2026}};
  • Hamilton-Zero, a ~0.5-billion-parameter neural tensor-network foundation model for ground states of quadratic qubit Hamiltonians, pretrained on hundreds of thousands of Hamiltonians, with zero-shot median errors of 4–13%; it replaces DMRG rather than seeding it {{heightman2026}}.

The reverse direction, MPS used to pretrain neural quantum states, is well developed and carries a cautionary number: 3–8× fewer optimization steps translated into at most 1.6× less wall-clock time {{kan2024}}. Hamiltonian-conditioned neural quantum states exist {{zhang2023,rende2025}}, as do conversion tools between neural and tensor-network states {{chen2018,fernandez2025}}, but none has been used to seed DMRG. The closest successful analogue elsewhere is a network that predicts the density matrix used to start DFT self-consistency {{hazra2024}}.

What theory allows

Finding MPS ground states is hard in the worst case {{eisert2006,schuch2008}}, so no initializer can carry a general guarantee. Isometric MPS energy landscapes have no barren plateaus: the gradient variance is system-size independent and scales as 1/m2 {{barthel2025}}. Random starts therefore fail because of discrete competing basins, not vanishing gradients. Inside the correct basin convergence is roughly geometric, so a start that is ten times closer saves only a handful of sweeps (our inference from local convergence theory). The payoff of a learned start lies in choosing the basin and in amortizing over families of Hamiltonians.

Objective and hypotheses

Objective. Build and benchmark a model that, given a lattice Hamiltonian and a target symmetry sector, outputs a few MPS seeds from which DMRG (a) reaches the correct ground-state basin with higher probability and (b) reaches a converged answer in less total time than the best non-learned baseline, counting inference, conversion and amortized training.

  1. H1. Time-to-solution and reliability are controlled by the basin, sector and correlation pattern of the start, not by its energy or fidelity.
  2. H2. A generator trained on a family of Hamiltonians can propose seeds that land in the correct basin more often than random and physics-designed seeds, at a cost that is repaid after a modest number of instances.
  3. H3. Seeds that carry the correct quantum numbers on every bond make noise-free single-site DMRG, the cheapest variant per sweep, reliable for the final high-bond-dimension sweeps.

Approach

The work is organized in five work packages. WP1 calibrates the problem without any machine learning, WP2 builds the generator shown in Figure 1, and WP3–WP5 test it on progressively harder and larger problem classes.

WP1. Calibration: how much does the start matter? (no ML)

No published study plots sweeps or wall time against start quality for one Hamiltonian, or measures how often random starts get stuck as a function of model, size and algorithm. We will take converged reference states and degrade them in controlled ways: truncation to χ0 ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}, added noise, and substitution of a wrong-basin state. Each start is run under fixed schedules with two-site DMRG plus noise, single-site DMRG with subspace expansion, and controlled bond expansion, each with and without noise. We record sweeps and wall time to the convergence criteria in Section 5, plus the seed-to-seed spread of random starts.

The result shows whether the dependence is logarithmic, as local theory suggests, or has thresholds at basin boundaries. It bounds the gain any learned start can deliver and tells us which Hamiltonians are worth training on. The harness built here is reused for every later comparison.

WP2. Generator design

Input. The lattice as a graph (sites, bonds and their couplings, fields), the MPS path through it, global parameters such as filling and cylinder width, and target sector labels: particle number, Sz or SU(2) label, momentum, spinon parity and chirality sign.

Output. K seeds (for example K = 4–8) with exact quantum-number labels on every bond and complex amplitudes, at modest bond dimension χ0 ≤ 32. We will compare three routes, in order of risk:

  • Route A, ansatz parameters (lowest risk). The network predicts parameters of a physically interpretable state, such as mean-field hoppings and pairings, a flux pattern, or a pinning pattern. The state is converted to an MPS with the Gaussian-MPS construction {{fishman2015}} or Gutzwiller projection {{jin2021}}. This keeps results comparable with the parton literature and should transfer across sizes, but it inherits the ansatz’s blind spots.
  • Route B, direct MPS tensors. A graph or transformer network emits symmetric block-sparse site tensors along the path. The seed’s energy is computed exactly by contraction, without Monte Carlo.
  • Route C, via a neural quantum state. A Hamiltonian-conditioned NQS {{zhang2023,rende2025}} is compressed to an MPS by tensor cross interpolation {{fernandez2025}}. It is the most expressive route and has the costliest conversion.

Training. The loss is a sum of local energy terms, since global-fidelity losses produce barren plateaus in MPS-based learning {{liu2022}}. A diversity term keeps the K seeds in different basins, and optional distillation uses DMRG solutions on narrow or short systems. DMRG then runs a short schedule from each seed and keeps the lowest extrapolated energy, which replaces the hand-run multi-start search.

WP3. Basin selection on affordable testbeds

We start with problems that have published random-start failures and cheap reference solutions: the mixed-field Ising chain near its first-order line and the ZXZ+XX chain with fourfold edge degeneracy at N = 32 {{block2021}}, and the 6×6 Kitaev cylinder, which has published random and Gutzwiller numbers to beat {{jin2021}}. The main metric is the probability of reaching the correct basin or flux sector, followed by time-to-solution and the final bond dimension needed.

WP4. Competing orders on 2D cylinders

The physics payoff is on doped Hubbard and t-J cylinders at 1/8–1/5 doping, trained on width 4 and tested on widths 6 and 8 {{ehlers2017,jiang2021,shen2024}}, and on kagome and triangular cylinders in both sectors {{yan2011,gong2014}}. The questions are whether learned seeds transfer across widths, where the physics itself changes, and whether they shorten the metastable episodes that burn high-bond-dimension sweeps.

WP5. Amortization over families (optional)

Disorder ensembles (random-bond XXZ and random-singlet chains) and dense parameter scans have many instances and no converged neighbour for a new realization {{xavier2018}}. They are the cleanest test of whether one trained model beats continuation and reduced-basis surrogates {{brehmer2023}}. Quantum chemistry, where orbital ordering dominates, is out of scope for now but is a natural extension.

Time-to-solution requires four conditions at once: an energy-variance threshold, an energy extrapolated in truncation error, a converged entanglement entropy, and a converged target correlator. A low variance alone shows only that the state is close to some eigenstate {{itensorfaq}}, and fixed-bond-dimension energies can rank competing states wrongly {{ehlers2017}}.

Wall time is reported three ways: without network cost, with inference and MPS conversion, and with training cost amortized over a family. The last gives a break-even number of instances N*. This matters because the reverse-direction pretraining study turned 3–8× fewer iterations into at most 1.6× in wall time {{kan2024}}.

Reliability is the probability of landing in the correct basin or sector across seeds and instances. We also report the final bond dimension needed, anytime curves of energy error against cumulative time, medians and 90th-percentile tails (benefits should concentrate in hard instances), and results at unseen couplings, larger sizes and wider cylinders.

Validation. Runs are done with and without noise. First-order lines are approached from both sides to expose bias. We apply the symmetry-restoration test, since “if DMRG restores a symmetry explicitly broken by the initial state, one has strong evidence against that particular ordering scenario” {{stoudenmire2012}}. Competing states are always compared by extrapolated, not fixed-m, energies.

Plan, milestones and deliverables

The schedule below is indicative and assumes about one person-year of effort with access to a cluster for large-bond-dimension reference runs. Each gate is a go or no-go decision.

  • D1 (month 3): an open benchmark harness and a dataset of reference MPS and timings.
  • D2 (month 4): paper 1, a systematic study of how DMRG time-to-solution and reliability depend on the starting state.
  • D3 (month 12): paper 2, learned, sector-conditioned seeds for DMRG, with code.

Points to discuss

  1. Target models. Which systems in Table 1 matter most for your current work? Are there problems where you already run multi-start or pinning searches that we could use as test cases?
  2. Code base. ITensor, TeNPy, block2 or an in-house code. We need U(1) and SU(2) symmetries, complex arithmetic, subspace or controlled bond expansion, and access to per-sweep timings.
  3. Compute and data. Cluster access for reference runs, and any converged MPS from past projects that could seed the training set.
  4. Division of work. A possible split: model selection, physical validation and large-m reference runs on one side; the benchmark harness and the generator on the other.
  5. Scope of the first paper. Whether WP1 alone, which needs no ML, is worth publishing early.
